Agentic AI

τ²-Bench: Evaluating Conversational Agents in a Dual-Control Environment

Sierra Research (arXiv)published 2025-06paper35 minworking

When an AI agent is tested in a lab setting where it alone controls all the tools, it can look highly capable — but real customer service and telecom scenarios involve a customer who can also take actions mid-conversation. This paper finds that adding an active human on the other side of the interaction causes success rates to collapse, in one case from about 74% down to 34% for the same underlying model. The bottleneck isn't the AI's reasoning — it's coordinating with an unpredictable second party who's also acting.

Read if you sell or evaluate conversational agents for support or telecom use cases; skip if your interest is model architecture, not deployment realism.

Memory for Autonomous LLM Agents: Mechanisms, Evaluation, and Emerging Frontiers

arXiv (Pengfei Du)published 2026-03paper50 minworking

This paper builds a shared vocabulary for what 'agent memory' actually means, separating it clearly from just having a bigger context window. It breaks memory into how long information is kept, where it's stored, and how the agent decides what to write, keep, or retrieve — then surveys five different technical approaches to doing that well. The core point: an agent's ability to remember and improve over time is a design choice with real engineering tradeoffs, not a side effect of using a more powerful model.

Read if you need precise language to explain 'does it remember me' to a technical or skeptical buyer; skip if you just need the one-paragraph version, which is above.

AI in Banking

SR 26-2: Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management (supersedes SR 11-7)

Federal Reserve Board of Governors (with OCC and FDIC)published 2026-04docs15 minworking

US banking regulators just replaced their fifteen-year-old model risk rulebook with a more flexible, principles-based framework — but they explicitly excluded generative and agentic AI from it, calling those systems too new and fast-moving to regulate yet. That means banks deploying agentic AI today are doing so under improvised extensions of old guidance, not a finished rule, while a formal industry-wide review is still pending. For anyone selling into a bank's model risk or compliance function, this is the difference between 'here are the rules' and 'we're figuring out the rules as we go.'

Read if you sell any AI or model-driven tool into a US bank's risk, compliance, or model validation team; skip if your buyer is purely on the business/product side.

Why Capital One built its multi-agent AI platform around open-weight models

VentureBeatpublished 2026-08article8 minworking

Capital One's ML engineering leadership explains why they didn't buy an off-the-shelf agent platform: no vendor combination hit their targets for cost, latency, and quality at the same time. Instead they fine-tuned open-weight models themselves, built their own system for coordinating multiple agents, and layered in their own governance controls. Their edge came from owning that full stack, not from picking a better vendor.

Read if you're pitching a foundation-model API or agent platform to a large bank with serious engineering resources; skip if your target buyer is a smaller institution without an in-house ML org.

EU AI Act's High-Risk Deadline: Deferred, Not Cancelled

Cloud Security Alliancepublished 2026-08article10 minworking

A newly enacted EU regulation pushes the deadline for high-risk AI obligations — including credit scoring systems — from August 2026 to December 2027, giving banks sixteen extra months before those rules bite. But it's not a full reprieve: transparency requirements and obligations on general-purpose AI providers stay on the original timeline. Banks that read this as 'AI Act compliance can wait' are only half right.

Read if you're selling AI or compliance tooling into EU banks and need to know how urgent the buyer's timeline actually is; skip if your business is US-only.
